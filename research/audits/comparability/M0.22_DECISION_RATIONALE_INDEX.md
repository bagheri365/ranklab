## 1. Why this index exists

RankLab reached its frozen protocol through a sequence of audits and staged
decisions. Those records are intentionally detailed and chronological, but a
future reviewer should not have to reconstruct the project's entire reasoning
from more than twenty milestone documents.

This index therefore records, for each major choice:

- the frozen decision;
- the principal rationale;
- important rejected alternatives or caveats;
- the existing M0 document containing the detailed evidence.

Where a short rationale here and a detailed milestone document differ in
specificity, the detailed frozen milestone document is authoritative.

## 3. Rationale notes reviewers are likely to ask about

### 3.1 Why are standard and randomized evaluation pairs not intersected?

The research question concerns whether model assessment changes under different
logged exposure policies. Standard and randomized logging therefore naturally
produce different exposed user-item pairs.

RankLab matches the primary regimes on shared users and shared videos, but does
not require the exact user-item pair to appear in both regimes.

Requiring pair intersection would answer a narrower question about only the
subset of exposures common to both policies and would remove a meaningful part
of the regime difference the project is designed to study.

This is why M0.15 explicitly freezes:

`pair_intersection_required: false`.

The shared-tab and tab-1 analyses remain sensitivities rather than replacements
for this primary estimand.

References:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.15_EVALUATION_SUPPORT_FREEZE.md`
- `research/audits/logging_regimes/M0.3_SUPPORT_SCENARIO_AUDIT.md`

### 3.2 Why was `long_view` never used for tuning?

RankLab asks whether conclusions about model quality are stable not only across
logging regimes but also across behavioral targets.

If `long_view` were used to choose architectures, hyperparameters, epochs, or
seeds, target B would no longer provide a clean second evaluation criterion.

Models are therefore fitted and selected using `is_click` only. The exact same
frozen checkpoints are evaluated against both `is_click` and `long_view`.

This choice deliberately trades target-specific optimization for a cleaner
comparison of behavioral-target sensitivity.

References:

- `research/audits/targets/M0.4_TARGET_SEMANTICS_AUDIT.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.7_TRAINING_CONTRACT_RATIONALE.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.19_EVALUATION_SEMANTICS_FREEZE.md`

### 3.3 Why exclude zero-relevance users rather than assign NDCG=0?

For a user with no relevant candidate under a target, IDCG is zero, so NDCG is
not mathematically defined.

Assigning zero would be convenient computationally but would blend two
different phenomena:

1. whether the candidate set contains an observed positive for the target;
2. how well a model ranks relevant items when ranking quality is defined.

The post-eligibility audit showed that zero-relevance rates differ substantially
by regime and target, especially for randomized `long_view`.

RankLab therefore excludes zero-relevance users from the target-specific macro
NDCG denominator and requires their counts/fractions to be reported separately.

Reference:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.19_EVALUATION_SEMANTICS_FREEZE.md`

### 3.4 Why is the minimum candidate count 2 instead of 10?

After frozen support and training-index eligibility:

- standard primary users: 22,882;
- standard users with at least 2 candidates: 21,643;
- standard users with at least 10 candidates: 10,435;
- randomized primary users with at least 2 candidates: 22,882.

A 10-candidate minimum would therefore discard more than half the standard
population and would create a much stronger regime-specific selection effect.

A one-candidate list contains no meaningful ranking choice.

The minimum was consequently frozen at 2, while NDCG@10 simply uses all
available candidates for lists shorter than 10.

References:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.16_CANDIDATE_STRUCTURE_AUDIT_PROTOCOL.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.19_EVALUATION_SEMANTICS_FREEZE.md`

### 3.5 Why separate seed variability from user-bootstrap uncertainty?

BPR and LightGCN have five frozen training seeds. Those seeds describe
optimization/training stochasticity.

The primary metric, however, is a macro average over users, and the main
population uncertainty is therefore naturally clustered at the user level.

RankLab consequently:

- reports all seed-specific stochastic-model results;
- reports seed SD/range/order agreement;
- averages frozen seeds for the model point estimate;
- performs the primary population bootstrap over users.

This avoids pretending five training seeds are five independent samples of the
evaluation population.

Reference:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.20_STATISTICAL_INFERENCE_FREEZE.md`

### 3.6 Why is there no practical NDCG threshold?

No externally justified RankLab-specific NDCG difference was available before
M1.

Choosing a threshold after observing test-set effects would create an
outcome-dependent decision rule and weaken the preregistration logic.

RankLab therefore reports raw NDCG effects and confidence intervals without
relabeling small values as practically equivalent using a post-hoc epsilon.

Reference:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.20_STATISTICAL_INFERENCE_FREEZE.md`

### 3.7 Why are tab restrictions sensitivities only?

The audit established shared numeric tab IDs, but the public dataset semantics
do not safely justify assigning richer UI meanings to every numeric tab value.

Furthermore, restricting to shared tabs or tab 1 materially changes standard
evaluation coverage while barely changing the randomized primary population.

RankLab therefore avoids undocumented scenario semantics in the primary
analysis and uses:

- shared users/videos as primary support;
- shared users/videos/shared tabs as sensitivity A;
- shared users/videos/tab 1 as descriptive sensitivity B.

References:

- `research/audits/logging_regimes/M0.3_SUPPORT_SCENARIO_AUDIT.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.15_EVALUATION_SUPPORT_FREEZE.md`

### 3.8 Why is the dataset SHA a RankLab manifest digest?

The project audited six source CSVs individually and retained their exact
SHA-256 values.

The upstream dataset does not provide a semantic release identifier that
RankLab can safely treat as a formal version. M0.21 therefore defines an
internal canonical dataset identity by hashing a filename-sorted manifest of
those six audited file hashes.

This aggregate digest is explicitly a RankLab provenance mechanism. It is not
presented as an upstream KuaiRand release checksum.

References:

- `research/audits/logging_regimes/M0.1_DATA_PROVENANCE.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.21_FINAL_PROTOCOL_FREEZE.md`

### 3.9 Why is tuning seed 0 also part of the five primary seeds?

Seed 0 was the single predeclared tuning seed used for model-selection
experiments. The later primary replication policy froze seeds 0–4 and retained
seed 0 rather than silently replacing it after tuning.

This creates a known asymmetry: one of the five primary training seeds was also
the tuning seed.

RankLab handles this by disclosure rather than by changing the seed set after
the fact. Hyperparameters and fixed endpoints are not re-selected separately
for seeds 1–4.

The seed-specific results remain visible in M1 so any unusual seed-0 behavior
can be inspected directly.

References:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.11_PRIMARY_MULTI_SEED_POLICY.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.13_PRIMARY_CHECKPOINT_FREEZE.md`

### 3.10 Why do historical checkpoint protocol hashes differ from the final M0 hash?

Training artifacts were created after the training contract and model-selection
rules were frozen but before later evaluation-support, candidate, and inference
rules were added to the overall registry.

Their historical protocol hashes therefore identify the training-stage
protocol state under which those artifacts were created.

M0.21's final hash:

`9bd72ae2d3042dc9511734563b90b8668519b401b847ce218f706a465cc41a32`

covers the complete final M0 registry, including decisions that do not alter
model fitting.

The historical training hashes should not be rewritten retroactively. Doing so
would falsely imply that the checkpoint bytes were produced under a registry
state that did not yet exist.

References:

- `research/audits/comparability/M0.13_PRIMARY_CHECKPOINT_FREEZE.md`
- `research/audits/comparability/M0.21_FINAL_PROTOCOL_FREEZE.md`

## 4. Important limitations frozen rather than hidden

M0 intentionally preserves several limitations for transparent reporting.

### BPR grid resolution

The selected BPR configuration won by a very small validation-NDCG margin.
RankLab therefore treats the exact winning configuration as the frozen
predeclared selection result, not as evidence that nearby configurations are
meaningfully inferior.

### LightGCN regularization search

The formal LightGCN grid varied dimensions, layer count, and learning rate but
held regularization fixed at 0.0001. This asymmetry relative to BPR is a known
model-selection limitation and must be disclosed rather than repaired after
test-set evaluation.

### Logging-regime comparability

Shared user/video support improves comparability but does not make the two
logging regimes exchangeable. Exposure distributions, target prevalence, and
candidate-set sizes remain materially different.

### Outcome-conditioned NDCG denominator

Excluding zero-relevance users is mathematically motivated, but it means the
target-specific NDCG population can differ by regime and target. RankLab
therefore freezes both native and matched-user cross-cell analyses.

### No causal interpretation

RankLab studies the stability of offline recommender evaluation under different
logged exposure regimes and targets. The frozen design does not, by itself,
identify causal effects of changing a production recommender policy.
